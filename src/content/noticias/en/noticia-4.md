<p><font size="4">New research led by scientists at the Texas A&amp;M University College of Engineering has yielded promising results for the future of space colonization: the successful cultivation of <i>Pisum sativum</i> (garden peas) in Martian regolith simulant. The secret to this success, however, is unconventional: the use of "frass"—excrement and waste—from black soldier fly (<i>Hermetia illucens</i>) larvae as fertilizer.</font></p><p><img src="images/noticias/noticia-4-imagem-1.jpg"></p><p><font size="2">Image 1. <i>Hermetia illucens</i> – Black soldier fly. Insect that decomposes organic matter. Source: Embrapa.</font></p><h3>The Martian Soil Challenge</h3><p><font size="4">Unlike Earth, the surface of Mars is covered in regolith, a material devoid of organic matter, microorganisms, or essential nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. For long-duration missions, which can last between 15 and 35 months, reliance on supplies from Earth is unfeasible, making <i>in situ</i> food production a critical necessity.</font></p><h3><i>Frass: The Solution Suggested by the Study</i></h3><p><font size="4">The study cites a previous experiment that quantified the increase in nutrients available to plants when Martian regolith simulant was treated with black soldier fly larvae. The recorded increases were:</font></p><p><font size="4">Potassium: <b>+1179</b></font></p><p><font size="4">Phosphorus: <b>+803%</b></font></p><p><font size="4">Magnesium: <b>+67%</b></font></p><p><font size="4">Manganese: <b>+7847%</b></font></p><p><font size="4">Nitrogen: +188% (rising from 19.8 ppm to 57.1 ppm)</font></p><p><font size="4">The researchers tested different concentrations of frass (0%, 10%, 25%, and 50%) mixed with Martian regolith simulant (MMS-1).</font></p><p><img src="images/noticias/noticia-4-imagem-2.jpg"></p><p>Image 2. Frass: Insect excrement. Source: U.S. Department of Agriculture.</p><h3>Germination: Intrinsic Viability of the Substrate</h3><p><font size="4">The study demonstrated that, although the Martian regolith simulant (MMS-1) lacks organic matter and macronutrients, it did not hinder the initial growth phase of the pea plants.</font></p><p><font size="4">Absence of Statistical Difference: The Wilcoxon test (p = 0.313) confirmed that there was no significant difference between germination rates in regolith and commercial soil. This indicates that the pea seed's own energy reserves are sufficient for initial establishment, regardless of the medium's nutritional poverty.</font></p><p><font size="4">Optimization: Using quadratic regression models, the study predicted that the optimal frass level to specifically maximize germination in regolith is 6.9%, suggesting that even a small organic amendment optimizes initial success.</font></p><h3>Height vs. Productivity: The Growth Paradox</h3><p><font size="4">Although the plants in the regolith exhibited greater heights, the article explains that this did not directly translate into greater overall vigor.</font></p><p><font size="4">Etiolation or Vigor? On average, pea plants in regolith were numerically taller across all frass levels. However, the study highlights that final dry biomass (weight) was significantly lower in regolith than in commercial soil at the same frass concentrations (p = 0.0064).</font></p><p><font size="4">This difference suggests a trade-off: plants in the regolith may have invested more energy in vertical growth (possibly seeking light or due to stress) at the expense of biomass accumulation and fruit development.</font></p><h3>Chlorophyll Content and Stress</h3><p><font size="4">This is one of the most technical points of the article, as it reveals how the plant processes nutrients in a simulated environment.</font></p><p><font size="4">Breakdown of Negative Correlation: In ordinary soils, the study observed a significant negative correlation (R = -0.48) between height and chlorophyll: taller plants tended to be less green, signaling nutritional stress or nutrient dilution.</font></p><p><font size="4">Stability in Regolith: Surprisingly, plants grown in regolith maintained constant and higher chlorophyll levels, regardless of height (R = -0.081). This suggests that frass-treated regolith enables nutrient uptake or a stress response that deviates from conventional terrestrial agricultural patterns, maintaining stable photosynthetic capacity even under adverse conditions.</font></p><h3>Study Conclusion</h3><p><font size="4">Statistical analysis suggests that the optimal range for cultivation is an inclusion rate of 5% to 32% frass. This is higher than what is required for commercial soil because the regolith must first reach a minimum fertility threshold. Above 50%, nitrogen toxicity or excess salts becomes the limiting factor, inhibiting growth in both soil and regolith.</font></p><h3>Next Steps in Astrobotany</h3><p><font size="4">Although the simulant used does not contain perchlorates (toxic salts present in actual Martian soil), the research proves that, with appropriate treatment, Martian soil can support plant life.</font></p><p><font size="4">In addition to helping future astronauts, scientists state that this "insect fertilizer" technology could be applied on Earth to restore degraded or nutrient-poor soils, promoting more circular and sustainable agriculture.</font></p>
