<p><font size="4">As space exploration advances toward long-duration missions, the transition from "food sent from Earth" to "food produced <i>in situ</i>" is becoming a strategic necessity. A recent study led by Rebeca Gonçalves and published in <b>PLOS ONE</b> has provided new insights into optimizing this production through intercropping in Martian regolith simulants.</font></p><p><img src="images/noticias/noticia-3-imagem-5.png"><font size="2">Figure 1. Comparison between intercropping and monoculture treatments in a Martian regolith simulator. Gonçalves R. et al. (2024)</font></p><font size="4">Unlike traditional monoculture, intercropping involves the simultaneous cultivation of different species in the same space. For astrobotany enthusiasts, the concept goes beyond saving space; it is about seeking niche complementarity and resource facilitation.</font><h3><font size="5">The Experiment: Tomatoes, Peas, and Carrots in MMS-1</font></h3><h3><font size="4" style="font-weight: normal;">The researchers selected three species with complementary nutritional and horticultural properties: tomato (<i>Solanum lycopersicum</i>), pea (<i>Pisum sativum</i>), and carrot (<i>Daucus carota</i>). The goal was to test whether these plants could "help each other" in the MMS-1 regolith simulant (developed by NASA's JPL).</font></h3><p><img src="images/noticias/noticia-3-imagem-2.png"></p><p><font size="2">Image 2. In the experimental model, various combinations were created using three different types of substrate (Martian regolith, sand, and soil) and three plant species (tomato, pea, and carrot). Source: Gonçalves R. et al. (2024).</font></p><h3><font size="5">Results: The Critical Factor of Symbiosis</font></h3><p><font size="4">The data revealed a dichotomy: in the Mars simulant (MMS-1), the intercropping system showed a yield disadvantage, with a Relative Yield Total (RYT) of 0.93. Tomatoes benefited, but the performance of peas and carrots dropped drastically. In contrast, in river sand (the control), the intercropping was a success, with an RYT of 1.32, indicating that the plants produced 32% more when grown together than when grown separately.</font></p><img src="images/noticias/noticia-3-imagem-4.png"><font size="2">Figure 3. Comparison of productivity between cultivation treatments and soil types. Source: Gonçalves et al. (2024).</font><h3><font size="4">Why the difference?</font></h3><p><font size="4">The answer lies in root nodulation. In Martian regolith, nodule formation on the pea plants was virtually nonexistent. Without biological nitrogen fixation, the pea plant shifted from being a "facilitator" to a "competitor" for scarce resources, intensifying pressure on the carrot and being outcompeted by the tomato, which proved to be a dominant species with a "voracious" appetite for nutrients.</font></p><h3><font size="5">Regolith Obstacles: pH and Compaction</font></h3><img src="images/noticias/noticia-3-imagem-6.jpg"><font size="2">Image 4. Panoramic photo of the surface of Mars. Source: NASA.</font><p><font size="4">The failure of nodulation in MMS-1 was attributed to the following intrinsic factors of the simulated Martian regolith:</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>1. High pH (~8.5):</b> The alkaline environment hinders the survival of <i>Rhizobium</i> bacteria and the bioavailability of essential nutrients such as iron.</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>2. Compaction:</b> MMS-1 consists of very fine particles that acquire a clay-like texture when wetted, reducing gas diffusion and water conductivity.</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>3. Sterility:</b> The lack of a diverse microbial community and organic matter impairs root health and nutrient uptake.</font></p><h3><font size="5">Pathways to Bioregenerative Agriculture</font></h3><p><font size="4">Despite the challenges posed by raw regolith, the success of the sand treatment demonstrates that the consortium is viable if soil conditions are improved. Researchers propose the following improvements for future colonies:</font></p><font size="4"><br><b>I. Soil improvement:</b> The use of inedible parts of early harvests as compost to lower the pH and improve soil structure.</font><p><font size="4"><b>II. Particle size selection:</b> Use regolith fractions with larger grains to avoid extreme compaction.</font></p><font size="4">This study demonstrates that agriculture on Mars will be a matter not just of botany, but of ecosystem engineering. Understanding the complex interactions between plants and microorganisms under extraterrestrial conditions is the key to transforming the red dust into a productive garden.</font>
