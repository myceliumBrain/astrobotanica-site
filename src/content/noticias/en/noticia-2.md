<p><font size="4">Long-duration space exploration is moving away from a model of total reliance on Earth-based supplies in favor of an integrated resource management architecture. A recent study proposes a framework that unifies In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) and Bioregenerative Life Support Systems (BLSS), treating them as subsystems that are functionally distinct yet architecturally coupled. While ISRU focuses on acquiring and converting external materials (such as atmospheric CO₂ and regolith ice), BLSS ensure internal cycle closure by regenerating air, water, and nutrients from habitat waste streams. This synergy is crucial, as ISRU-derived products sustain the biological modules, which, in turn, reduce the demand for continuous external extraction.<img src="images/noticias/noticia-2-imagem-1.png" alt=""></font><font size="2">Image 1. Schematic representation of the coupled functions of in situ resource utilization (ISRU) and a bioregenerative life support system (BLSS) within the framework of a long-duration exploration architecture.</font></p><h3><font size="5">The Food Ecosystem: Beyond Caloric Survival</font></h3><p><font size="4">Within this architecture, food production systems evolve from mere cargo storage units into critical interface components. Although food production is not strictly an ISRU function, biological systems rely directly on ISRU flows to obtain gases, water, and mineral nutrients. The challenge lies in mitigating the nutritional degradation of pre-packaged diets through the regeneration of edible biomass in closed-loop cycles.</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>Photobioreactors: Microalgae and Cyanobacteria</b></font></p><p><font size="4">Phototrophic microbial platforms offer a compact, fast-growing route for biomass production. Studies involving <i>Limnospira</i> indica PCC8005 have demonstrated biomass productivities of up to 0.58 g L⁻¹ d⁻¹, with an oxygen production rate of 1.19 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹. It is estimated that <i>Chlorella</i>-based systems could replace up to 35% of an astronaut's daily food mass, making them economically advantageous for missions lasting between 4 and 7 years.</font></p><p><font size="4"><img src="images/noticias/noticia-2-imagem-2.png" alt=""></font><font size="2">Image 2. Examples of closed or semi-closed photobioreactors for microalgae cultivation. Source: Masojídek, Jiří &amp; Torzillo, Giuseppe (2008)</font></p><h3><font size="5">New innovations: Use of local inputs</font></h3><p><font size="4"><b>I. Regolith as Fertilizer:</b> Strains such as <i>Anabaena sp.</i> PCC 7938 and <i>Chroococcidiopsis</i> have demonstrated the ability to grow using lunar and Martian regolith leachates as a source of minerals.</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>II. Nitrogen Recycling:</b> The use of synthetic human urine as a nitrogen source for cyanobacteria has been validated, allowing the resulting biomass to serve as a substrate for other heterotrophic components of the BLSS.</font></p><h3><font size="5">Higher Plant Cultivation Systems</font></h3><p><font size="4">While microorganisms ensure nutritional density and volumetric efficiency, higher plants provide dietary variety, fiber, and essential psychological benefits against sensory monotony.</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>VEGGIE:</b> Developed by NASA, this modular system utilizes LED light and an open architecture that allows plants to interact directly with the cabin environment. Its effectiveness lies in its simplicity and the support it provides for crew well-being.</font></p><p><font size="4"><img src="images/noticias/noticia-2-imagem-3.png" alt=""></font><font size="2">Image 3. Astronaut Serena Auñón-Chancellor harvests Red Russian kale and Dragoon lettuce from the Veggie system on November 28, 2018. Source: NASA.</font></p><p><font size="4"><b>Advanced Plant Habitat (APH):</b> Represents the state of the art in automation, enabling remote control of complex variables such as spectral quality, light intensity, and CO₂ and ethylene concentrations. The APH has already demonstrated the capability to complete entire life cycles (seed-to-seed) for wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) in microgravity.</font></p><p><font size="4"><img src="images/noticias/noticia-2-imagem-4.png" alt=""></font><font size="2">Image 4. John “JC” Carver, a payload integration engineer with the Kennedy Space Center Test and Operations Support Contract, opens the growth chamber door of the Advanced Plant Habitat (APH) Flight Unit No. 1 for a test harvest of half of the <i>Arabidopsis thaliana</i> plants growing inside. Source: NASA</font></p><font size="4"><b>LADA Greenhouse:</b> Of Russian origin, this system focuses on nutrient delivery via capillarity and has been instrumental in studying the morphological and biochemical responses of crops under reduced gravity, demonstrating high operational reliability on the ISS.</font><p><font size="4"><img src="images/noticias/noticia-2-imagem-5.png" alt=""></font><font size="2">Image 5. Astronaut beside the LADA greenhouse, installed in the ISS Zvezda module since 2002. Source: Banhatti, Dilip G &amp; Banhatti, Radha. (2013)</font></p><h3><font size="5">Integration and Scaling Challenges</font></h3><p><font size="4">The transition from isolated experiments to an integrated surface architecture still faces technological bottlenecks. Full integration requires that the thermal extraction of water from regolith be followed by rigorous purification, as volatile contaminants and metals could inhibit bacterial or plant growth. Projects such as MELiSSA aim to mimic terrestrial biogeochemical cycles through interconnected compartments, where organic waste is liquefied and nitrified to feed cultivation chambers for higher plants and algae.</font></p><font size="4">The study concludes that space sustainability will not stem from a single technology, but rather from defined microbial consortia and hybrid systems that coordinate energy and mass flows between abiotic machines and biological organisms.</font>
